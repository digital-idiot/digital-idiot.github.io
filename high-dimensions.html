High Dimensions: Why Models Lift Their Data

Seven views of one idea, and its catch
→→→→→→
1 of 7 · Tangled

A straight line can't separate these classes

Each data point has two features \((x_1,x_2)\) and a class, A or B. A linear classifier can only split the feature space with one straight line.

\[ \hat y=\operatorname{sign}(w\cdot x+b) \] \[ \text{boundary: } w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b=0\ \ (\text{a line}) \]
line accuracy
majority class

Drag the two round handles to move the line. The side that classifies more points correctly is taken as class A.

Even the best line is stuck: for the circle and the ring it ties always predicting the majority class, and for XOR and the spirals it stalls around 65–70%. The fix is not a cleverer line: it is one more feature.

2 of 7 · Lift

Add a dimension and the line becomes a circle

Give each point a third feature, its squared distance from the centre. In 3‑D the class A points sit at the bottom of a bowl, and a flat plane slices them off.

\[ \phi(x)=\big(x_1,\ x_2,\ z\big) \] \[ z=x_1^2+x_2^2\quad\text{or}\quad z=x_1x_2 \] \[ z=h\ \Rightarrow\ x_1^2+x_2^2=h\ \ (\text{a circle}) \]

The right extra feature makes the problem linear. But we had to guess it, and no single guess fixes the ring or the spirals. Next: don't guess, add many random features.

3 of 7 · Width

Many random features: an untrained hidden layer

Lift each point into \(D\) dimensions with random ReLU units, then fit only a linear readout on top. Each unit is a ramp across the input space; a weighted sum of many ramps can bend the boundary into any shape.

\[ \phi_j(x)=\max\!\big(0,\ u_j\cdot x-c_j\big),\quad j=1\ldots D \] \[ f(x)=\textstyle\sum_j w_j\,\phi_j(x)+b \] \[ w=(\Phi^{\top}\Phi+\lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}y \]

test accuracy

The \(u_j, c_j\) are random and never trained: only the readout \(w\) is fitted. Pick the dataset here or in chapter 1.

Width alone turns a hard problem into a linear one. This is why hidden layers are wide, and why kernel methods work. How much room does width give, exactly?

4 of 7 · Capacity

Cover's theorem: how many labelings a plane can fit

Scatter \(N\) points in \(d\) dimensions and colour them at random. How often can one flat cut separate the colours? Almost always while \(N<2(d+1)\), almost never after: a sharp edge.

\[ P(N,d)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{N-1}{k} \] \[ P=\tfrac12\ \text{ at }\ N=2(d+1) \]

Trials use a perceptron with an iteration cap, so near the edge they can slightly undercount. The right panel checks every labeling of \(N\) points in 2‑D exhaustively.

More dimensions, more patterns a linear readout can express, including random ones. That is power, and also the ability to memorise noise. Hold that thought for chapter 6.

5 of 7 · Room

In high dimensions, random directions are almost perpendicular

Each feature a network learns is a direction in feature space; each direction can stand for one concept. In 2‑D, two random directions can point anywhere. In 768‑D they are nearly always close to 90° apart, so thousands of concepts fit without interfering.

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{u\cdot v}{\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert},\qquad \operatorname{std}(\cos\theta)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt d} \]

This is why feature vectors are long, such as the 768 numbers of a ViT token: room for many nearly independent features. Now the catch.

6 of 7 · The catch

Raw high‑dimensional space is a trap

In many dimensions, every point is about equally far from every other, and almost all the volume sits near the edges. "Nearest neighbour" stops meaning "most similar".

\[ \frac{d_{\max}-d_{\min}}{d_{\min}}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\to\infty\ }\ 0 \] \[ \text{volume within } 5\%\text{ of the surface}=1-0.9^{d} \]

400 random points in a unit cube of \(d\) dimensions; distances measured from one of them.

So high dimensions help only if the dimensions are meaningful. Next: the same problem on real images, and the fix.

7 of 7 · Features

Good features beat raw dimensions

Each 24×24 image is a point in 576‑D pixel space. Search for the images closest to a query, first by raw pixel distance, then by just two shape features.

\[ d_{\text{pix}}(a,b)=\lVert a-b\rVert,\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{576} \] \[ f(a)=\big(\varphi_1,\ \text{extent}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{2} \] \[ \varphi_1=\frac{\mu_{20}+\mu_{02}}{A^2} \] \[ \text{extent}=\frac{A}{\text{box area}} \]

Spread, Hu's first moment invariant \(\varphi_1\): how far the shape's area sits from its centre, divided by area² so it ignores position and size (\(\mu\): central moments, \(A\): area). Fill, or extent: how much of its bounding box the shape covers (circle ≈ 0.79, square ≈ 1, triangle ≈ 0.5).

The orange dots on every result mark where the query shape sits. Wrong‑shape matches by pixels sit right on top of it: same place, different shape. The correct shapes are shifted, so pixel distance calls them far.

Raw pixel distance measures where things are, not what they are. A trained feature extractor learns a space where distance means similarity: real data lives on a low‑dimensional manifold inside its raw space. Bahri et al. (2021) link this to scaling laws: loss falls roughly as \(N^{-4/d}\), where \(d\) is the data's intrinsic dimension.
